Subject: The Permanent Way — a mythic civil engineering game set on the Great Eastern

Dear Great Eastern Railway Society,

I am writing in the hope the Society might be interested in a new project, The Permanent Way (https://github.com/splippers/thepermanentway). 

It is a mythic-industrial game and cinematic universe in which Isambard Kingdom Brunel is resurrected after a catastrophic HS2 blunder tears open a portal to the under-infrastructure of Britain. Rather than focusing on operating trains, the game focuses on the difficult bit: surveying abandoned routes, inspecting surviving structures, acquiring land, negotiating with owners and councils, and working out how to bring a lost line back to life.

The first corridor is the Great Eastern's Witham–Maldon–South Woodham Ferrers route. The repository already contains a researched, source-referenced mission covering its nine stops, with a LIDAR-traced alignment, structure survey (including bridge 839 at Cold Norton), period photographs and tools to trace the trackbed. I've recently added three "mandates" to the mission — Restorationist (come what may), Engineer (inspect and choose per structure), and Surveyor (full planning) — with field links to Google Maps/Street View for checking structures on the ground, and a hidden "bill" epilogue for those who force the line through.

My aim is not to rewrite the history of the GER, but to use careful research to make players care about the viaducts, cuttings, halts and bridges that were swept away, and why reopening them is harder than simply relaying rail. Where the facts are uncertain I say so, and the project cites its sources.

Two specific questions have come up researching Maldon West that the written sources don't answer, and that I suspect a member might simply know: comparing a photograph of the booking office and its twin covered footbridge stairways against one from the April 1957 steam special (J67 No. 68628), the tower and the glazed footbridge corridor look substantially damaged by 1957, not just decayed — does anyone know what happened, and when? And with that footbridge apparently out of use, how was the platform reached in the station's closed years — a surviving route from the road, or simply along the trackbed from the goods yard through the tunnel, which stayed open for goods traffic until the late 1950s? One more, unconfirmed: the undamaged photograph itself looks like it may be from a disused-stations archive, going by its filename, but I haven't been able to reach the site to check — does anyone recognise it, or know roughly when it was taken?

I would be very grateful for any corrections, drawings, photographs, memories or pointers the Society might be willing to share. If there is interest, I'd be happy to send a build link, show the current Mission 1 in more detail, or provide material for the Journal/News. Equally, if the Society would prefer I do not reference it publicly, I will respect that.

Many thanks for the work you do preserving the GER.
